# Slice 10 — Matchday feel and a football capability check

The user asked for a less serious, more immersive broadcast, longer celebrations, less surrounding UI, clearer explanations and better stadium sound. They also reported that dribbling and aerial play seemed absent. During the work they identified the synthetic crowd/goal voice as unpleasant, approved the revised whistle, and asked for more epic drums. Thought bubbles remain an optional future experiment; no invented player thoughts are displayed.

## Broadcast changes

The stadium occupies the complete watch area, with a compact team/score/clock overlay and one **Inside the match** button. The previous title, model-name header and event footer bands are removed. Floating controls contain play, rewind, time and sound; speed, player numbers, volume and fullscreen are under **Viewing options**. The whole top-down pitch remains visible, with letterboxing when viewport and stadium proportions differ. Desktop inspection stays beside the pitch; narrow screens use an overlay.

Inspection starts with **Team plans**, **What they see**, **Rules** and **Matches**. Plain-language explanations come first, while exact prompts, raw observations, instructions and generation details remain available under disclosures. Scripted practice and incomplete footage retain honest labels. The interface never describes recorded observations as a live model token stream.

Goals now receive six seconds of watch time at normal speed, including several seconds in a settled huddle. The timeline and seeking show the longer actual viewing duration. This adds 8.3 seconds to the existing two-goal match, making it 89.183 seconds to watch; football time, score and recorded actions do not change. [Decision 007](../decisions/007-BROADCAST-TIME.md) explains the mapping and its numeric boundaries.

The ball's height is easier to read: its drawn rise is 12 pixels per physical metre rather than five, while the ground shadow always marks the exact physical x/y. Its shadow broadens and fades with altitude. The ball also visibly rolls when carried. None of this changes its height, reach, contact or trajectory in the engine.

All six trees now sway in staggered gusts, with different upper/lower canopy motion and small leaf movement. Their trunks and ground shadows stay fixed. Only those fixed parts belong to the cached stadium; moving canopies are drawn fresh over it, so they cannot leave ghost images. Wind follows watch time and reduced motion retains static foliage. The original tree positions and pitch geometry are unchanged.

## Football findings

The current model match contains 60 decisions where the acting team owned the ball. Of those, 49 immediately kick or shoot, ten give Coral's stationary keeper another guard order, and only one orders an outfield move. That sole run, Cyan #2, successfully carries the ball 5.6 metres in the next second. The mechanic works; the controllers barely choose it.

Of 58 executed kicks/shots, 57 use zero loft. The lone loft-3 pass peaks at 0.594 metres, below the 0.65-metre foot-control threshold and only 2.42 old logical pixels above ground. The maximum height in the whole match is a 1.697-metre throw-in. There is no evidence here that gravity or carrying needs replacement.

The new **Running & chips** scripted practice shows Coral #7 carrying 9.994 metres, chipping over a stationary defender at two seconds, and #9 receiving at about 3.22 seconds before running onward. The eight-second fixture has hash `a4588f70`; the chip peaks at 1.993 metres and has no deflection. Its explicit scripted orders demonstrate capabilities and verify the renderer. They are not a new model-played result.

For future generation, the shared prompt now distinguishes **carry, pass or shoot**: permission to kick is not an instruction to release possession, and a carrier's move order dribbles with the ball. It asks the keeper to adapt after repeated interceptions and explains loft as upward velocity with approximate height examples. There is no automatic receiver selection, marking, spreading, chip or dribble decision. The published model match retains its exact original prompt and actions. No new paid generation or model migration was performed in this slice.

OpenAI describes the current GPT-5 nano baseline as its cheapest/fastest GPT-5 option, with summarization and classification as example uses ([official model documentation](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/models/gpt-5-nano), checked 8 September 2026). Controlling eleven footballers is a different task; the observed choices justify a controlled comparison, not a claim that a particular replacement will play better. Next, compare controllers on identical carry/pass/chip and keeper-distribution snapshots, then run a short match with the most promising configuration.

## Sound and verification

The approved whistle is preserved. Continuous hiss, harmonic fake crowd voices, and synthetic clap/applause layers are removed. Deeper bass drums, resonant higher hits, accents, pauses and rolling fills form a 130 BPM rhythm. Fractional step timing preserves that tempo on the 60 Hz timeline. Percussion plays during open play at 1×; other speeds retain selected event sounds without accelerating the drum rhythm. Goals use a short, locally bundled CC0 crowd recording at its original pitch; an unavailable asset skips the cheer. Saves do not trigger synthetic applause. [Sound credits](../SOUND-CREDITS.md) record the source/license and the user's reference links, which were not auditioned or included in the site.

Browser audio defaults to enabled, but its AudioContext is created/resumed by the first play gesture; browsers restrict unsolicited audible playback ([Chrome Web Audio policy](https://developer.chrome.com/blog/web-audio-autoplay)). Mute remains available before starting and is respected by subsequent play actions. Samples are prepared once, voices are bounded, and pause, mute, seek or hidden tabs stop them. The natural cheer loads asynchronously once and never triggers a past goal when it arrives. The final whistle finishes after automatic full time. Clock events and physical incidents use their correct frame boundaries; abandonment uses the actual final frame because its timestamp differs between a timeout and an in-step dismissal.

Strict TypeScript, all 98 tests across thirteen files, Prettier and whitespace checks pass. The production build succeeds at 392.14 kB JavaScript (123.33 kB gzipped). The published model recording still validates and independently replays to hash `31ab92f4`, score 0–2 and 3,600 playing ticks. Eleven public/build files, including decompressed match exports, were checked against both configured provider credentials with no matches.

At 737×813 the browser shows the full pitch, compact overlays, accessible viewing options and simplified inspector. Space on the options disclosure opens/closes it without starting playback. The actual model match plays through at 1× to FULL TIME 01:00, 0–2 and the exact 89.183-second watch endpoint. Seeking into the longer goal shows the visible scoring-side huddle beneath the banner. The new carrying/chipping practice also plays through normally to its exact eight-second endpoint and stays labelled scripted. The complete scripted fixture's slider maximum reaches exact full time at 103.8 watch seconds; both the numeric mapping and the actual input handler have regression tests. A second regression covers an incident at tick 122: converting its visible frame 123 through seconds would yield 122.99999999999999, so timeline discovery now uses the exact recorded frame instead.

Direct tree draw-call checks confirm varied/repeatable canopies, fixed trunks, static reduced motion and no overlap with the playing field; the rendered contact sheet was visually inspected. Audio checks cover finite deterministic synthesis, quiet sample boundaries, event timing and browser lifecycle. The procedural preview at the default volume peaks at 0.1798 with no clipped samples; this checks signal bounds, not subjective sound quality. User listening feedback established the whistle approval and removal of the objectionable crowd/clap layers; no agent listening assessment is claimed.

The engine and all saved outcomes remain unchanged. Known limits include simplified football, very little model-selected dribbling/loft in the existing recording, and no direct evidence yet that the new prompt changes controller behaviour. These are the next football evaluation questions, rather than presentation problems to hide.
